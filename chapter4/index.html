Facts, assumptions and a valuation date

You are preparing a short valuation note on Allianz SE. Start with a common set of assumptions so that you can compare calculations. Then develop and defend your own scenario.

Allianz's capital management policy targets a regular dividend payout of 60% of adjusted attributable net income and additional capital returns, including buybacks. 01 / 4 · About 7 minutes

From the last dividend to today's model value

Hint 1 · The cash-flow date matters

D₀ is already paid and is not part of the future cash flows. Grow it once to obtain D₁.

Hint 2 · Gordon growth model

D₁ = D₀ × (1 + g)
P₀ = D₁ / (r − g), requiring r > g

Show worked solution · Base case

D₁ = €17.442. P₀ = 17.442 / (0.06 − 0.02) = €436.05. This is €56.05 above the benchmark. It is a conditional model result, not proof of mispricing or a risk-free arbitrage opportunity.

02 / 4 · About 6 minutes

How much do the assumptions matter?

Complete the four missing model values. Every cell starts from D₀ = €17.10 and uses its own g to calculate D₁. Values already shown provide reference points.

Hint · Look at both the numerator and denominator

A new g changes D₁ as well as r − g. With positive dividends, the discounted infinite sum converges only if r > g. A finite period of growth above r can still be modelled separately.

Show worked solution · Sensitivity

A small change in r or g can move the valuation substantially. None of these scenarios is a probability-weighted forecast.

03 / 4 · About 6 minutes

Work backwards from the price

Keep D₀ = €17.10 and r = 6%, and suppose the model must match the €380 reference price. What constant dividend growth rate would that require?

Hint 1 · D₁ also depends on the unknown growth rate

Write 380 = 17.10 × (1 + g) / (0.06 − g). Do not keep the base-case D₁ fixed when solving for g.

Hint 2 · Rearrange and cross-check

g = (P₀ × r − D₀) / (P₀ + D₀)
Dividend yield = D₁ / P₀
Under constant growth: expected capital gain rate = g

Dividend yield plus expected capital gain rate should equal r. This is a model expectation, not a guaranteed realised return.

Show worked solution · Implied growth

The inferred rate depends on the assumed cost of equity. The market price alone cannot identify both r and g.

Separate near-term growth from long-run growth

Use an alternative teaching scenario: dividends grow at 8% for the next three payments, then at 2% forever. Keep r = 6%. This is a hypothetical dividend path, not a translation of Allianz's EPS guidance into a dividend forecast.

Hint 1 · Where does the terminal value sit?

The perpetual 2% growth phase begins with D₄. Its value sits at t = 3, immediately after D₃ has been paid. The terminal value represents D₄ onwards; it does not include D₃ again.

Hint 2 · Discount both components

Dₜ = 17.10 × 1.08ᵗ for t = 1, 2, 3
D₄ = D₃ × 1.02
P₃ = D₄ / (0.06 − 0.02)
P₀ = D₁/1.06 + D₂/1.06² + (D₃ + P₃)/1.06³

Show worked solution · Two-stage model

The higher value follows from the assumed dividend path. A more elaborate model is not automatically more accurate. Much of the estimated value still depends on long-run assumptions.

Prompts for your discussion
  • Why might medium-term earnings growth differ from sustainable dividend growth?
  • How could insurance losses, capital requirements or payout decisions change the dividend path?
  • Buybacks can affect future dividends per share. Why would adding total buybacks directly to a per-share dividend risk mixing units or double counting?
  • Why is a bond's promised yield not automatically the appropriate discount rate for equity?

Use the linked company sources as evidence, not as a ready-made estimate of r or g. A cost of equity is an estimate. Historical dividend growth and management targets alone do not determine its value.

Optional bond extension · About 7 minutes

Different cash flows, different discount rate

Consider a fictional corporate bond, not an actual Allianz security: face value €1,000, annual coupon rate 4%, three years to maturity, and a market YTM of 5% annually. Value it immediately after a coupon date. Assume promised payments are made. Use the stated bond YTM for pricing; do not substitute the 6% equity cost of capital.

Hint · Include principal at maturity

P = 40/(1 + y) + 40/(1 + y)² + 1,040/(1 + y)³

Change y from 5% to 6% while keeping cash flows and maturity fixed.

Show worked solution · Bond

The coupon rate is below the 5% YTM, so the bond trades at a discount. A higher required yield lowers its price. A coupon is a contractual promised payment; an equity dividend depends on future results and payout decisions. Comparing the bond YTM with the stock's dividend yield alone does not establish which investment is preferable.
