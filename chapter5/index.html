The brief

A five-year launch in Mainz

Green Wheels plans to operate 700 dockless bicycles. Customers pay a monthly subscription plus a low usage charge. Use the following fixed teaching assumptions, adapted from the course’s Bike Sharing case. They are not current market estimates.

Model conventions: read before building
  • Year 1 starts with the full customer base. Every customer is billed for 12 months. Do not round forecast customer counts. The 1% starting share assumes Green Wheels wins all heavy users described in the original case.
  • All recurring cash flows occur at year-end. Bicycles and app development are paid at date 0. All prices are net of VAT. Ignore inflation, working capital, financing cash flows and replacement investment. No residual value.
  • The app expense creates an immediate tax saving at date 0. Assume it can be offset against other taxable profits immediately. This is a deliberate teaching assumption; a stand-alone loss-making start-up may not receive that benefit immediately.
  • Record expenses and capex as positive amounts in the spreadsheet. Subtract them in EBIT and FCF formulas. Tax expense at date 0 is negative because it is a tax benefit.

In the checks below, use no thousands separators. Decimal points and commas are accepted. Enter rates as percentages (12.34 means 12.34%). Keep spreadsheet formulas at full precision; money checks allow €1 of rounding difference.

Gate 1 · 10–12 minutes

Turn market assumptions into revenue

  1. Complete the missing assumptions in Inputs!C6:C23. Enter 1% as 1%.
  2. In Model, inspect the example formulas in E16, E17 and F16. Fill the customer and ride formulas across to year 5. Use fixed references such as Inputs!$C$9 for assumptions.
  3. Build subscription revenue, ride revenue and total revenue in rows 18–20. Multiply by the correct time units before copying across.

Hint 1 · Translate the units

1.5 hours contains three half-hours. Each customer pays 12 monthly fees and makes 120 rides per year. Apply 2% growth four times to get from year 1 to year 5.

Hint 2 · Example formulas

E18: =E16*Inputs!$C$12*Inputs!$C$14
E19: =E17*(Inputs!$C$10/Inputs!$C$11)*Inputs!$C$13
E20: =SUM(E18:E19)

German Excel uses SUMME instead of SUM. Copy these formulas to the right. The year references move; the assumption references stay fixed.

Pause with your partner: click a year-5 revenue cell and explain every reference. The lecturer may ask you to show the formula, not only the result.

Gate 2 · 12–15 minutes

Bridge operating profit to cash flow

Complete Model rows 22–33 for date 0 and years 1–5. Use the notes beside the model. Administration and support stay constant; maintenance follows the number of rides.

EBIT = revenue − operating expenses − depreciation
Tax expense = EBIT × 35%
FCF = EBIT − tax expense + depreciation − capex

The app is an operating expense at date 0. Bicycle depreciation begins in year 1. The app must not be charged again in year 1 or added back as depreciation.

Hint · The initial cash flow

Date-0 EBIT = −app expense
Date-0 tax expense = −app expense × tax rate
Date-0 FCF = −app expense × (1 − tax rate) − bicycle capex

For year 1, calculate 700 × €350 / 5 for depreciation. It reduces EBIT, then is added back to cash flow. Year-1 FCF is therefore larger than profit after tax.

Discuss: why is buying bicycles not an immediate EBIT expense, and why is depreciation added back? Which assumption makes the immediate tax saving possible?

Gate 3 · 10–12 minutes

Apply the investment rules

  1. Calculate discount factors, present values and cumulative undiscounted cash flows in Model rows 35–37.
  2. Calculate NPV using the built-in function in E7, and independently sum all present values in E10. Calculate IRR in E8 using date 0 through year 5.
  3. In E9, report the first whole year-end with nonnegative cumulative cash flow. The board’s payback cutoff is 2 years or less.

Hint 1 · The Excel NPV timing trap

The NPV function treats the first value as a cash flow one period from today. Add date-0 FCF separately. IRR, however, includes date 0 in its cash-flow range.

Hint 2 · English and German function names

English: =NPV(Inputs!C22,E33:I33)+D33
German: =NBW(Inputs!C22;E33:I33)+D33
English: =IRR(D33:I33)
German: =IKV(D33:I33)
Cross-check: =SUM(D36:I36) / =SUMME(D36:I36)

Argument separators and function language depend on your application settings. Payback can be determined from the cumulative row; you do not need a complicated search formula.

Discuss: do NPV, IRR and the two-year payback rule agree here? Why should payback still not be your main value-creation criterion?

Gate 4 · 10–12 minutes

Test the business case

Use the Review sheet. First record base NPV. Then change one input at a time, copy the resulting NPV as a value into the snapshot table, and restore the original input before the next test.

  • Customer share: 0.8% instead of 1.0% (20% fewer starting customers).
  • Maintenance cost: €0.45 instead of €0.33 per ride.
  • Discount rate: 12% instead of 8%.

These are recorded results, not a live sensitivity table. Re-run them if you change the model. This simple workflow works across spreadsheet applications without Excel-specific Data Tables.

Hint · Verify that your model responds

A customer-share change affects subscriptions, rides, usage revenue and maintenance. A cost-per-ride change affects maintenance only. The discount-rate change affects valuation, not operating cash flow. If nothing changes, inspect hardcoded numbers and missing cell links.

For discussion, not automatically graded. Numerical gates do not assess formula quality or your written reasoning.

Restore the base assumptions and save/export your workbook. Explain why a high model IRR is not evidence that customers will actually sign up. The tested changes have different sizes, so their NPV impacts are not a universal ranking of parameter sensitivity.

Optional extension / preparation for discussion

Where would the recommendation change?

Complete the NPV profile in Review and create a scatter chart. Compare its zero crossing with the IRR. Try a starting customer share of 0.4%: can the project still create value?

Find one real bike-sharing competitor and record the source and access date. Compare subscription fees, trip duration caps and usage charges. Do not replace the base-case inputs during the numerical gates.

Check whether the fleet can support expected rides per day and peak-hour demand. Identify costs omitted here, such as theft, insurance or bicycle replacement. Do not mistake average capacity for peak capacity.
